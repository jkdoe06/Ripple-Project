## 1. R5B Essay Framework

R5B focuses on **analytical writing** — not summary, not opinion, but **argument-driven analysis with textual evidence.**

### The R5B Formula
```
Observation → Argument → Evidence → Analysis → So What?
```

Every paragraph should:
1. Make a **claim** (topic sentence)
2. Provide **evidence** (quote or specific reference)
3. **Analyze** the evidence (explain HOW it supports your claim)
4. Connect back to **thesis** (why this matters)

### What Instructors Are Looking For
- Original, non-obvious thesis
- Close engagement with the text (quotes, specific passages)
- Analysis (not summary)
- Clear paragraph structure with transitions
- Revision and polish

## 2. Close Reading Method

### Step-by-Step Close Reading

1. **Read the passage twice** — first for content, second for craft
2. **Mark patterns:** repeated words, images, sounds, structures
3. **Note contrasts:** what's being set against what?
4. **Identify tone shifts:** where does the mood change?
5. **Ask "why this word?"** — what other word could the author have used?
6. **Context:** how does this passage relate to the whole work?

### What to Analyze in a Passage

| Element | Questions to Ask |
|---------|-----------------|
| Diction | Why this specific word? Connotation? Register (formal/informal)? |
| Imagery | What senses are engaged? What's the dominant image? |
| Syntax | Short or long sentences? Fragments? Parallelism? |
| Tone | What's the emotional register? Does it shift? |
| Figurative language | Metaphor, simile, personification — what's being compared to what? |
| Narrative voice | Who's speaking? What do they know/not know? Reliable? |
| Structure | Why does this passage come at this point in the text? |

## 3. Thesis Statement Templates

### Strong Thesis Formula
```
Although [common reading / surface interpretation],
[text/author] actually [your non-obvious argument]
through [specific technique/pattern],
which reveals [deeper meaning / "so what?"].
```

### Examples

**Weak:** "The story uses symbolism."
**Strong:** "Although the snowstorm in X appears to represent death, its recurring association with silence actually mirrors the protagonist's deliberate withdrawal from language, suggesting that isolation is not imposed but chosen."

**Weak:** "The author writes about loneliness."
**Strong:** "Through the systematic collapse of domestic space — rooms shrinking, walls closing, furniture disappearing — Y constructs loneliness not as an emotional state but as a physical architecture that the protagonist builds around herself."

### Thesis Checklist
- [ ] Is it arguable? (Could someone disagree?)
- [ ] Is it specific? (Does it name texts, techniques, or patterns?)
- [ ] Is it analytical? (Goes beyond summary or opinion?)
- [ ] Does it have a "so what?" (Why does this matter?)
- [ ] Can you support it with textual evidence?

## 4. Literary Analysis Toolkit

### Narrative Techniques

| Technique | Definition | Effect |
|-----------|-----------|--------|
| First-person | Narrator is a character ("I") | Intimacy, unreliability |
| Third-person limited | Narrator knows one character's thoughts | Focus, suspense |
| Third-person omniscient | Narrator knows all | Dramatic irony, scope |
| Free indirect discourse | Third-person that sounds like character's thoughts | Blurs narrator/character boundary |
| Unreliable narrator | Narrator whose account is questionable | Reader must interpret independently |
| Stream of consciousness | Unfiltered flow of thoughts | Interiority, fragmentation |
| Frame narrative | Story within a story | Distance, multiple perspectives |

### Figurative Language

| Device | Example | Effect |
|--------|---------|--------|
| Metaphor | "The world is a stage" | Direct comparison, transforms meaning |
| Simile | "Cold as a Scandinavian winter" | Comparison with like/as |
| Personification | "The fjord swallowed the boat" | Gives nature agency |
| Synecdoche | "All hands on deck" | Part for whole |
| Metonymy | "The crown decreed" | Associated thing for the thing |
| Irony (dramatic) | Reader knows what character doesn't | Tension, pathos |
| Irony (situational) | Outcome opposite of expected | Surprise, critique |

### Structural Devices

| Device | What It Does |
|--------|-------------|
| In medias res | Starts in the middle of action |
| Flashback | Interrupts chronology to show past |
| Foreshadowing | Hints at what's to come |
| Parallelism | Repeated structure for emphasis |
| Juxtaposition | Placing contrasting elements side by side |
| Motif | Recurring image/idea throughout text |
| Symbol | Object/image that represents something beyond itself |

## 5. Scandinavian Literature Themes

### Major Themes in Nordic Literature

| Theme | Description | Common In |
|-------|------------|-----------|
| **Nature vs. Civilization** | The wild as both threat and liberation | Hamsun, Lagerlöf |
| **Isolation & Interiority** | Characters alone with their thoughts | Ibsen, Strindberg |
| **Social Conformity** | Pressure to fit Jante Law ("you're not special") | Ibsen, Sandemose |
| **Gender & Power** | Women's roles, domestic confinement | Ibsen (A Doll's House), Undset |
| **Existential Dread** | Anxiety, meaninglessness, death | Kierkegaard, Hamsun |
| **Light & Darkness** | Literal (Arctic seasons) and metaphorical | Throughout Nordic lit |
| **Modernity & Tradition** | Old ways colliding with industrial/urban life | Lagerlöf, Hamsun |
| **Silence & Communication** | What's unsaid matters more than what's said | Bergman, Ibsen |
| **Guilt & Redemption** | Protestant/Lutheran moral weight | Throughout |
| **The Uncanny (det uhyggelige)** | Familiar made strange, folk horror | Scandinavian Gothic |

### Jante Law (Janteloven) — Cultural Context
Ten rules of Nordic social conformity:
1. You're not to think you are anything special
2. You're not to think you are as good as us
3. You're not to think you are smarter than us
...etc.

**Why it matters for analysis:** Many Scandinavian texts critique or embody this collective suppression of individuality.

## 6. Essay Structure Templates

### 5-Paragraph Analytical Essay (Standard R5B)

```
¶1 — Introduction
  - Hook (striking quote, question, or observation)
  - Context (author, text, relevant background)
  - Thesis statement

¶2 — First Analytical Point
  - Topic sentence (claim)
  - Evidence (quote + citation)
  - Analysis (2-3 sentences explaining HOW evidence supports claim)
  - Transition to next point

¶3 — Second Analytical Point (deeper or different angle)
  - Same structure as ¶2
  - Builds on or complicates the first point

¶4 — Third Analytical Point / Counterargument
  - Address potential objection OR
  - Offer most complex analysis
  - Show nuance

¶5 — Conclusion
  - Restate thesis in new language
  - Zoom out: broader implications ("so what?")
  - End with a strong final sentence (not a summary)
```

### Research Paper (Longer R5B assignment)

Add:
- Literature review section (what scholars have said)
- Multiple primary texts compared
- More developed counterargument
- Works Cited page (MLA format)

## 7. Citation & MLA Format

### In-Text Citations
- Author and page: `(Ibsen 42)`
- No author: `("Title" 42)`
- Multiple works by same author: `(Ibsen, A Doll's House 42)`

### Integrating Quotes

**Short (< 4 lines):** Integrate into your sentence with quotation marks.

> Nora describes her secret as "something to be proud of" (Ibsen 12), inverting the expected shame.

**Long (4+ lines):** Block quote, indented 1 inch, no quotation marks.

### Works Cited Format

**Book:**
Last, First. *Title*. Publisher, Year.

**Article:**
Last, First. "Article Title." *Journal*, vol. X, no. Y, Year, pp. XX-XX.

## 8. Common Feedback & How to Fix It

| Feedback | What It Means | Fix |
|----------|--------------|-----|
| "Too much summary" | You're retelling the plot | Replace summary with analysis: ask "how" and "why" |
| "Needs a stronger thesis" | Thesis is obvious or vague | Use the thesis template; make it arguable and specific |
| "More evidence needed" | Claims without textual support | Add a quote for every claim |
| "Analyze the quote" | You dropped a quote and moved on | After every quote, write 2-3 sentences explaining HOW it works |
| "Unclear argument" | Paragraphs don't connect | Make sure each ¶ has a topic sentence that links to thesis |
| "Awkward phrasing" | Sentences are hard to follow | Read aloud; break long sentences; cut filler words |
| "So what?" | No larger significance | End paragraphs and conclusion by explaining why your point matters |

## 9. Writing Process Checklist

### Before Writing
- [ ] Read the text at least twice
- [ ] Annotate passages that stand out
- [ ] Identify 2-3 patterns or tensions
- [ ] Draft a working thesis
- [ ] Create an outline

### While Writing
- [ ] Every paragraph has: claim → evidence → analysis
- [ ] Quotes are integrated smoothly (not dropped in)
- [ ] Transitions connect paragraphs logically
- [ ] You're analyzing, not summarizing

### After Writing (Revision)
- [ ] Read aloud — mark anything that sounds off
- [ ] Check: does every paragraph support the thesis?
- [ ] Cut filler words (very, really, basically, essentially)
- [ ] Vary sentence length (mix short and long)
- [ ] Check MLA formatting and citations
- [ ] Proofread for grammar and typos

## 10. Vocabulary & Key Terms

### Literary Criticism Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Discourse | The way language constructs meaning |
| Subtext | Meaning beneath the surface dialogue/action |
| Agency | A character's ability to act and make choices |
| Binary | Opposition between two concepts (light/dark, nature/culture) |
| Hegemony | Dominant cultural/social power structures |
| Liminality | Being on a threshold, in-between states |
| Intertextuality | How texts reference or echo other texts |
| Ekphrasis | Description of a visual artwork in writing |
| Allegory | Extended metaphor where characters/events represent abstract ideas |
| Catharsis | Emotional release through art |
